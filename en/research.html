ASNM is a decision-support system for first-time founders. It asks you to commit your predictions in writing, then shows you how far those predictions sit from what a transparent, reproducible model returns. The gap is the product.

The problem

The most expensive decisions get made when the founder knows least

Pricing, the first two hires, which market to enter — these get decided in month one, when there is no evidence to reason from and no cheap way to be wrong. Meanwhile the research on founder overconfidence is unusually consistent: entrepreneurs predict their own survival far above realized rates, and the gap does not close much with experience.

Most tools respond by giving founders more information. ASNM starts elsewhere — it makes the founder's own assumptions explicit, recorded, and comparable against a model whose reasoning is fully visible. What matters is not the model's number. It is the distance between that number and the one the founder produced from memory, and whether that distance narrows over time.

Why not just ask a chatbot

Not another answer engine

General-purpose AI generates answers. ASNM is designed to structure, preserve, and compare the decision process around those answers.

Founder prediction captured before any model output is shown
Transparent, reproducible scoring — no randomness, published weights
Calibration gap recorded and revisitable over time
Venture state and decisions linked longitudinally
The long-term asset is not an AI-generated answer. It is a purpose-built longitudinal dataset linking what founders believed before a decision, what they chose, how the venture state changed, and what ultimately happened.
Evidence

What holds up, and what doesn't yet

I would rather state this plainly than have you go looking for it. Everything below marks where the evidence currently stops.

Supported
  • Adoption pathway (SEM, n=190, Korean sample). Preliminary evidence supports the proposed pathway: cognitive-bias awareness → perceived usefulness → adoption intention. Indirect effect .785, 95% CI [.679, .903]; PU→AI β=.949, p<.001.
  • Usability. 15-participant pilot including accelerator directors, a national startup-agency researcher, and a VC analyst. SUS 82.2.
  • Working software. Modules 1–3 run end to end today — market exploration, founder orientation profiling, assumption elicitation, rule-based simulation, scored output, and report generation. Module 4 is the next technical development stage.
  • Transparency. The scoring rubric is published inside the product. No randomness: identical input returns identical output.
Not established
  • Predictive validity. The resilience score is a weighted rubric, not a trained or calibrated model. No claim is made anywhere in the product that it predicts venture survival.
  • Founder typology. Six orientations informed by prior literature. No psychometric validation has been conducted.
  • Decision quality. The study measured adoption-relevant properties, not whether decisions improve. That study has not been designed yet.
  • Generalization. All 190 respondents and all 15 pilot users are based in Korea. Replication in another market is untested.
The system

Four modules, one pipeline

01

Market discovery

Plain-language search across industry trends, plus a selectable trend interface returning comparative market sizing from cited public figures.

Working
02

Founder decision orientation profile

A ten-item exploratory instrument mapping how a founder tends to decide under uncertainty, across six orientations. A reflection framework, not a personality diagnosis.

Working
03

Assumption elicitation & venture simulation

The founder records a survival estimate and writes a pre-mortem before designing anything. The engine then returns a resilience score across six weighted factors, a risk profile, and the distance between prediction and rubric.

Working
04

Venture digital twin

A data replica of founder, venture, and market supporting counterfactual experiments. Architecture specified; engine is the next development stage.

Methodology

The score is a rubric, and the rubric is public

No black box, no trained weights, no randomness. Six factors, published openly, shown to the user alongside their result. The weights are author-defined initial values informed by the venture-failure literature — not empirically estimated coefficients. They are hypotheses to be calibrated against longitudinal outcome data, and that calibration is the open research problem.

FactorWhat it readsWeight
Capital runwayStarting capital against burn assumptions25
Team completenessCoverage of critical early roles20
CalibrationDistance between the founder's own estimate and the rubric20
Technical focusConcentration of scope — narrower scores higher15
Market definitionWhether a specific customer has actually been named10
Organizational baseRetention and operating-model choices10

Calibration carries a fifth of the total because founder–estimate error is central to ASNM's research hypothesis: that a founder who is roughly right about their own odds is in a different position from one who is thirty points optimistic. Whether this weighting predicts meaningful venture outcomes remains an empirical question to be tested.

Target architecture

The twin is the engine. Immersion is just the interface.

Digital Twin Engine→ Scenario Simulation→ AI Agents→ Immersive Layer

The twin represents a venture as state variables — capital, burn rate, MRR, CAC, headcount, market growth, competitive intensity, founder–market fit, validation level. The founder pulls a lever and reads what moves:

  • Hire a marketing lead now? Runway shortens, acquisition capacity rises, execution risk shifts.
  • Move price from $29 to $49? Revenue may rise, conversion may fall, break-even moves.
  • Enter a second market? TAM expands, regulatory exposure rises, capital requirement rises.

Module 3 implements this as a rule-based engine today. Turning it into a calibrated state-transition model estimated from longitudinal venture data is the central open technical problem — and the area where collaboration with researchers in longitudinal modeling would be most valuable.

Why one market first

Korea was the test bed, not the target

The empirical work was conducted in Korea because that is where I could reach 190 founders and run a pilot with people who actually operate accelerator programs. That makes it a strong first sample rather than a finished market thesis: high startup formation, unusually dense public support infrastructure, and a five-year survival rate around a third.

The mechanism under test — bias awareness driving perceived usefulness driving adoption — is not a claim about Korea. It is a claim about how founders relate to a tool that shows them their own overconfidence. The Korean sample provides the initial reference point for cross-market comparison. The next question is whether the observed mechanism replicates under different funding norms, institutional structures, and attitudes toward startup failure.

Commercial hypothesis

Who this is for, and who would pay

Primary users
Early-stage and first-time founders making high-uncertainty decisions.
Initial buyer hypothesis
Accelerators, university entrepreneurship programs, and public startup-support organizations.
Buyer problem
These organizations commit mentoring capacity, grants, program resources, and follow-on support before founder assumptions and decision risks have been systematically documented.
Proposed value
A structured pre-decision and longitudinal assessment layer that makes assumption gaps and venture risks easier to identify, discuss, and revisit across a cohort.
Path under validation
Institutional pilot → program licensing → B2B / B2G SaaS → decision-intelligence platform
These are commercial hypotheses, not validated claims. Buyer fit, willingness to pay, and repeat-use economics remain to be tested.
Next validation stage

Three paths, and the question of which comes first

I am currently evaluating three possible next paths. I would value guidance on which is best matched to ASNM's current stage.

01

Research collaboration

Replicate the behavioral findings in a U.S. context, and explore how the current rubric could be calibrated using longitudinal venture-outcome data.

02

Institutional pilot

Test ASNM with an accelerator, university entrepreneurship program, or startup-support organization to evaluate workflow fit, repeat use, and decision usefulness.

03

Commercial validation

Identify the economic buyer, willingness to pay, workflow integration, and a viable U.S. go-to-market path.

Selected references

What this work builds on

  • Åstebro, T., Herz, H., Nanda, R., & Weber, R. A. (2014). Seeking the roots of entrepreneurship: Insights from behavioral economics. Journal of Economic Perspectives, 28(3), 49–70. — entrepreneur overconfidence and entry decisions.
  • Cooper, A. C., Woo, C. Y., & Dunkelberg, W. C. (1988). Entrepreneurs' perceived chances for success. Journal of Business Venturing, 3(2), 97–108. — 81% of 2,994 surveyed founders rated their own odds of success at 7 in 10 or better.
  • Klein, G. (2007). Performing a project premortem. Harvard Business Review, 85(9), 18–19. — prospective hindsight as a method for surfacing failure modes before commitment.
  • Lichtenstein, S., Fischhoff, B., & Phillips, L. D. (1982). Calibration of probabilities: The state of the art to 1980. In Kahneman, Slovic, & Tversky (Eds.), Judgment Under Uncertainty. — the calibration framework underlying Module 3's prediction-gap factor.
  • Brooke, J. (1996). SUS: A "quick and dirty" usability scale. In Usability Evaluation in Industry. — instrument used in the 15-participant pilot (score: 82.2).
  • Statistics Korea. Business Demography Statistics (기업생멸행정통계), 2022 base year — five-year survival rate of new enterprises (34.7%), as reported by the Korea Institute of Startup & Entrepreneurship Development (KISED).
  • Lee, K. (2026). Adaptive Startup Navigation Model: An AI-agent decision-support system for early-stage founders. MSTM thesis, aSSIST. — source of the SEM results (n=190) and the pilot study reported here.
