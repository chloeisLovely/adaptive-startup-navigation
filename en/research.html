The problem

The most expensive decisions get made when the founder knows least

Pricing, the first two hires, which market to enter — these get decided in month one, when there is no evidence to reason from and no cheap way to be wrong. Meanwhile the research on founder overconfidence is unusually consistent: entrepreneurs predict their own survival at roughly twice the realized rate, and the gap barely moves with experience.

Most tools respond by giving founders more information. ASNM starts somewhere else — it makes the founder's own assumptions explicit, written down, and comparable against a model whose reasoning is fully visible. What matters is not the model's number. It's the distance between that number and the one the founder produced from memory.

Honest status

What holds up, and what doesn't yet

I'd rather state this plainly than have you go looking for it. Everything below is where the evidence currently stops.

Holds up
  • Adoption pathway (n=190, SEM). Cognitive-bias awareness → perceived usefulness → adoption intention. Indirect effect .785, 95% CI [.679, .903]; PU→AI β=.949, p<.001.
  • Usability. 15-participant pilot including accelerator directors, a national startup-agency researcher, and a VC analyst. SUS 82.2.
  • Working software. The full path runs end to end today — profiling, simulation, scored output, exportable report.
  • Transparency. The scoring rubric is published in the interface. Same input, same result, every time.
Doesn't yet
  • Survival prediction. The resilience score is a weighted rubric, not a trained model. No predictive validity is claimed anywhere in the product.
  • Founder typology. Six orientations informed by prior literature. No psychometric validation has been run.
  • Decision quality. The study measured adoption, not whether decisions actually improved. That study hasn't been designed yet.
  • Generalization beyond one market. All 190 respondents and all 15 pilot users are Korean. Whether the effect replicates elsewhere is an open question — and the one I most want to answer.

Methodology

The score is a rubric, and the rubric is public

No black box, no trained weights, no randomness. Six factors, published openly, shown to the user alongside their result. The weights are initial values drawn from the venture-failure literature; estimating them from outcome data is the next research step, not a claim I'm making now.

FactorWhat it readsWeight
Capital runwayStarting capital against burn assumptions25
Team completenessCoverage of critical early roles20
CalibrationDistance between the founder's own estimate and the rubric20
Technical focusConcentration of scope — narrower scores higher15
Market definitionWhether a specific customer has actually been named10
Organizational baseRetention and culture design10

Calibration carries a fifth of the total on purpose. A founder who is roughly right about their own odds is in a materially different position from one who is thirty points optimistic — regardless of which of them scores higher everywhere else.

Target architecture

The twin is the engine. Immersion is just the interface.

Digital Twin Engine→ Scenario Simulation→ AI Agents→ Immersive Layer

The twin represents a venture as state variables — capital, burn rate, MRR, CAC, headcount, market growth, competitive intensity, founder–market fit, validation level. The founder pulls a lever and reads what moves:

  • Hire a marketing lead now? Runway shortens, acquisition capacity rises, execution risk shifts.
  • Move price from $29 to $49? Revenue may rise, conversion may fall, break-even moves.
  • Enter a second market? TAM expands, regulatory exposure rises, capital requirement rises.

Module 3 implements this as a rule-based engine today. Turning it into a calibrated state-transition model is the open technical problem — and the part I'd most like to work on with people who do this rigorously.

Why one market first

Korea was the test bed, not the target

The empirical work was done in Korea because that's where I could reach 190 founders and run a pilot with people who actually run accelerator programs. That makes it a strong first sample rather than a finished market thesis: high startup formation, unusually dense public support infrastructure, and a five-year survival rate around a third.

The mechanism the study tested — bias awareness driving perceived usefulness driving adoption — isn't a claim about Korea. It's a claim about how founders relate to a tool that shows them their own overconfidence. Whether it holds in a market with different funding norms and a different failure culture is exactly the question a second sample would answer. The Korean data is the control condition, and I'm looking for the comparison.

What I'm looking for

Three questions, most useful first

  1. Does the problem exist here in the same form? If U.S. founders and university programs don't recognize this gap, I would rather learn that now than build against it for a year.
  2. Is there a path to validating the model with researchers at Stony Brook? The rubric needs outcome data to become an estimated model. That's a research problem before it's a product problem, and it's not one I can solve alone.
  3. Where does something at this stage actually belong? A research collaboration, a student-project pairing, an incubator track — I don't know which door is right, and being redirected is as useful to me as being introduced.
